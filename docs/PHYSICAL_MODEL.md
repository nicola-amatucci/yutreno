# A physical model railway driven by YuTreno: feasibility and first draft

**Status: idea, not started.** This document checks whether a model train can run under YuTreno's protection, with NFC tags as balises and a radio link as the RBC, and proposes a first draft of architecture, hardware and phases. Nothing here is built yet; the numbers marked as estimates must be measured in the first phase.

## The idea in one paragraph

Today YuTreno simulates everything: the train moves in `Simulation`, the balises are generated by the engine, the RBC is a function in the same process. In a model railway the train moves for real, so the engine stops being the source of the train's motion and becomes what it is for an external dynamics model: the rest of the world. The model train carries a small onboard unit that reads NFC tags glued between the sleepers (the balises), counts its own wheel turns (the odometry) and drives its motor; the RBC talks to it over WiFi. YuTreno keeps doing what it already does well: line, authorities, supervision curves, interlocking, scenarios, verdict and logs. Most of the pieces already exist, because the onboard and trackside protocols were designed to separate the onboard from the world and the interlocking from the field.

## Is it feasible?

Yes, at H0 scale and with a careful choice of components, and the riskiest point can be checked in a day. The analysis below goes through the five things that must work.

### 1. Scale, speeds and the line

Kinematics scales cleanly if lengths, speeds and accelerations are all divided by the scale factor and time is kept real: a braking distance v²/2a then scales like a length, and the curves computed by the onboard stay valid. At H0 (1:87):

| Real | H0 model |
| :-- | :-- |
| 160 km/h (44.4 m/s) | 0.51 m/s |
| 80 km/h | 0.26 m/s |
| service deceleration 1.0 m/s² | 11.5 mm/s² |
| 1 km of line | 11.5 m of track |
| balise group, 2 balises 3 m apart | two tags 34 mm apart |

A YuTreno line for the layout is therefore short: a 1 km line with two stations fits in an oval or a dog-bone of about 11–12 m of track, for example on a 2.4 × 1.2 m board. YuTreno already lets you draw such a line in the *Line* tab; the only addition is a scale factor applied at the boundary between engine and model (real metres ↔ model millimetres). At N (1:160) everything is twice as small and tight; at G (1:22.5, the scale of the ETCS test layout of TU Dresden) there is room for everything but speeds reach 2 m/s and the track is expensive. H0 is the recommended compromise: cheap, widely available, enough room for a test car.

### 2. NFC tags as balises

Real Eurobalises are passive transponders energised by the antenna under the train, which reads a telegram as it passes over them. NFC tags are the same idea at 13.56 MHz and a few centimetres of range, which is exactly what a model needs.

- **Tags**: NTAG213 stickers, 10–25 mm, glued on the sleepers between the rails. Each tag has a unique UID; the trackside configuration maps UID → (`NID_C`, `NID_BG`, `N_PIG`), so two tags 34 mm apart form a group and give the direction of travel, as in Subset-026. Optionally the NDEF memory can hold the telegram itself.
- **Reader**: RC522 (MFRC522, SPI, the cheapest), PN532 or PN5180 (longer range, interrupts). Mounted under a car with the antenna about 5–10 mm above the sleepers.
- **Reading time**: the critical number. With the common Arduino library, one `PICC_IsNewCardPresent()` poll of an RC522 takes about 27 ms. A tag is in the field over roughly 30–40 mm of travel, that is 60–80 ms at 0.5 m/s: two or three polls at the top scale speed, more at lower speeds. It is feasible but tight, which matches the experience reported by modellers: at large scale and high speed 13.56 MHz readers miss tags and 125 kHz readers with integral antennas work better; at small scale and modest speeds they work. Shorter timeouts, maximum antenna gain, or a reader with interrupt support (PN532/PN5180) improve the margin. This is the first thing to measure (phase 0).
- **Position at the passage**: the reading gives the odometer value at the moment of detection, with an error of up to one poll (≈ 14 mm at 0.5 m/s, ≈ 1.2 m real). It is the same kind of error that the confidence interval of the onboard already models: the national values `odo_error_fixed_m` and `odo_error_relative` just need realistic values.
- **Rails and metal**: nickel-silver rails are conductive and can detune a 13.56 MHz antenna; the tag must sit in the middle between the rails and the antenna be kept away from metal parts of the car. To be checked in phase 0 with the real track.

### 3. Odometry

Between two tags the onboard needs the distance travelled. A magnet on an axle and a Hall sensor give one pulse per wheel revolution (an H0 wheel of 11 mm has a 34.5 mm circumference, 3 m real per pulse): too coarse alone. An optical or magnetic encoder on the motor shaft gives tens of pulses per wheel revolution, or the axle can carry several magnets. Wheel slip and diameter tolerance accumulate an error that the tags reset at every group: it is precisely the ETCS principle of relocation on balises, and the model makes it visible.

### 4. Radio: the RBC over WiFi

The onboard unit is an ESP32, which has WiFi and Bluetooth on chip. Two ways to reach YuTreno:

- **WiFi to the PC**: the ESP32 joins a dedicated access point and keeps a WebSocket or UDP connection to a small YuTreno gateway. Typical round trips are a few milliseconds, with occasional spikes; WiFi power saving must be disabled on the ESP32, because it adds delays of up to the beacon interval.
- **ESP-NOW to a gateway ESP32** connected to the PC over USB: no access point, short frames, round trips typically around a millisecond. More robust in a crowded radio environment, at the price of a second board.

Either is far inside the budget: the onboard cycle of YuTreno is 100 ms, the RBC sends message 3 every 5 s or at every change, and [REAL_TIME.md](REAL_TIME.md) shows that the engine itself is hundreds of times faster than real time. The radio loss scenario can be made physical by switching the access point off.

### 5. The engine in real time

This is the only real change to YuTreno. The engine today runs in lockstep and waits for every external program; a model train does not wait. The engine therefore needs the clock-locked mode described as level 1 in [REAL_TIME.md](REAL_TIME.md): it steps with the clock, takes the latest measured position and speed of the model as the state of the train (exactly what an external dynamics model provides), uses the latest command of the onboard, and records every exchange with its time. Determinism is lost and replaced by recording and replay, which must be declared in any result obtained on the layout. The physical model is a small instance of level 3 of that page (physical signals), with the hard real time confined to the microcontroller: the ESP32 reads tags and pulses and drives the motor locally, and only messages travel over the radio.

## Architecture

```
               PC: YuTreno                                          layout
 ┌──────────────────────────────────────────┐        WiFi / ESP-NOW        ┌──────────────────────────────┐
 │ line, scenario, verdict, logs, page      │ ◄──── odometry, tags ──────── │ test car: ESP32 + NFC reader │
 │ reference onboard (phase 2)              │ ───── traction, brake ──────► │ encoder, motor driver        │
 │ RBC (simple or interlocking trackside)   │ ◄──── messages 136 ────────── │ (onboard EVC in phase 4)     │
 │ interlocking                             │ ───── messages 3, 16 ───────► │                              │
 │ physical gateway (new, src/server/)      │                               └──────────────────────────────┘
 │                                          │ ◄──── SCI-TDS occupancy ───── ┌──────────────────────────────┐
 │                                          │ ───── SCI-P point commands ─► │ object controllers: ESP32    │
 └──────────────────────────────────────────┘                               │ servo points, occupancy      │
                                                                            └──────────────────────────────┘
```

How the YuTreno concepts map onto the layout:

| YuTreno | Physical model |
| :-- | :-- |
| line chainage (m) | position along the track (mm) × scale |
| balise group, `NID_BG`, `N_PIG` | two NTAG213 tags, UID → identifiers in the trackside configuration |
| `odometry` of the onboard request | encoder pulses × wheel circumference |
| external dynamics response (`pos_m`, `vel_ms`) | measured position and speed of the test car |
| train commands (`traction`, `brake`, `emergency`) | PWM duty and braking ramp of the motor driver |
| RBC messages | JSON over WebSocket or ESP-NOW frames |
| SCI-P (`Cd_Move_Point`, `Msg_Point_Position`) | servo turnout with end-position microswitch |
| SCI-TDS (`Msg_TVPS_Occupancy_Status`) | current sensing on isolated rail sections, or IR sensors |
| `radio_loss` scenario | access point switched off for a while |

The trackside side is the most elegant part: the interlocking already speaks EULYNX telegrams to a simulated field, so a physical field is a set of small "object controllers" that speak the same JSON telegrams. The interlocking does not need to know whether the points are simulated or real.

## Where the onboard computer runs

Two stages, to keep the first one simple.

**Stage A — EVC on the PC.** The test car is a "smart bogie": it measures and actuates, nothing more. It sends odometry and tag readings every 50–100 ms and applies the traction and brake commands it receives. On the PC the gateway turns them into the onboard request of the reference onboard (odometry, balises with the odometer at the passage) and into the external dynamics response (position and speed), and turns the onboard outputs into motor commands. The RBC is in the same process. It is the shortest path to a train that stops by itself before the end of authority.

**Stage B — EVC on the ESP32.** The supervision moves onto the train, as in reality: the ESP32 runs an onboard derived from `examples/onboard/minimal_onboard.py` (rewritten in C++), reads the tags locally and commands the motor locally, and talks to the RBC on the PC only through radio messages 136, 3 and 16. This is the "RBC over WiFi" split of real ETCS level 2, and it is where the model becomes a teaching demonstrator of where each function lives. The reference onboard on the PC stays as the comparison.

## Draft bill of materials

Indicative prices, for one test car and a small layout; to be checked when buying.

| Item | Quantity | Indicative cost |
| :-- | :-: | --: |
| H0 track for an 11–12 m loop with two stations and two turnouts | 1 | €150–300 |
| H0 locomotive with a can motor (DC) and a freight or passenger car as test car | 1 + 1 | €80–200 |
| ESP32 board (small form factor for the car, for example an ESP32-C3 or S3 mini) | 1 | €5–10 |
| NFC reader RC522 (or PN532 / PN5180 for more margin) | 1 | €3–15 |
| NTAG213 stickers | 40–60 | €10–20 |
| motor driver (DRV8833 or TB6612) | 1 | €3–5 |
| encoder (magnetic or optical) or Hall sensor and magnets | 1 | €3–10 |
| power: constant DC on the rails with onboard regulation and a supercapacitor, or a small LiPo | 1 | €10–20 |
| turnout servos (SG90 or slow-motion) with end switches | 2 | €10–30 |
| occupancy detection (current sensors or IR) | 4–8 sections | €10–40 |
| ESP32 boards for the object controllers | 2 | €10–20 |
| dedicated WiFi access point (or a gateway ESP32 for ESP-NOW) | 1 | €0–30 |

A minimal first phase (one car, a straight or a loop, tags, reader, ESP32, PC) costs under €100 if a locomotive is already available.

## Phases

| Phase | Goal | What is built | Estimated effort |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-: |
| 0 | measure the risky numbers | reader on a car pushed by hand and by the loco at known speeds; tags on the real track; read rate vs speed, read position error, effect of the rails | 1–2 days |
| 1 | see the train in YuTreno | car sends tags and pulses over WiFi; a gateway shows the measured position on the line diagram of the page; no control | 3–5 days |
| 2 | closed loop, EVC on the PC | clock-locked mode of the engine; physical dynamics provider; motor commands from the reference onboard; the train stops before the EOA and at stations | 1–2 weeks |
| 3 | physical field | object controllers for two turnouts and four to eight sections, speaking SCI-P and SCI-TDS JSON to the interlocking; overtaking scenario on the layout | 1–2 weeks |
| 4 | EVC on the train | C++ onboard on the ESP32, RBC over WiFi; radio loss and emergency stop on the layout; comparison with the reference onboard on the same runs | 2–4 weeks |

On the YuTreno side the new code is: a scale factor in the line configuration, the clock-locked run loop (useful beyond the model), a "physical" dynamics provider and a gateway in `src/server/` that speaks to the ESP32s, and a configuration file mapping tag UIDs and object controllers to balise groups, points and sections. The protocols themselves do not change.

## Risks and how to handle them

- **Tags missed at speed.** Measured in phase 0; mitigations in order: lower top speed, shorter reader timeouts, PN532/PN5180 with interrupts, two readers in a row, larger tags. A missed tag is also a legitimate test case: it is a balise not read, which a real onboard must handle through linking.
- **Space in the locomotive.** At H0 the electronics go in a test car behind the locomotive, connected by a thin cable or by its own radio; at N it is hard, at 0 or G easy.
- **Power interruptions on dirty track.** They reset the ESP32: a supercapacitor or a small battery on the car, and track cleaning.
- **Odometry drift.** Expected and useful: relocation on tags is part of what the model should show; realistic national values for the odometry error make the confidence interval honest.
- **Radio spikes.** Power saving off, a dedicated access point or ESP-NOW; every exchange time-stamped so that each run states its timing quality.
- **Safety of the model.** The protection acts on a model, but a runaway train still falls off the board: an independent hardware stop (IR sensor before the buffers that cuts the motor power) is cheap and worth it.

## What it would show

A physical model makes visible what the simulator keeps abstract: the train locates itself only when it passes over a balise, its confidence interval grows between balises and shrinks at the next one, the RBC extends the authority as the interlocking sets routes, a missed message or a switched-off access point makes the onboard brake, a point that does not reach its end position keeps the authority short. For teaching it is a strong tool; for research it is a small hardware-in-the-loop bench on which the same scenarios of the simulator can be replayed with real timing, real odometry errors and real radio.
